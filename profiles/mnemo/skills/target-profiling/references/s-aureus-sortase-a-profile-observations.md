## Key new patterns

### 1. Cell-wall-shielded bacterial enzyme — the antibody-accessibility barrier that redirects the entire pipeline to small molecules

SrtA is the first profiled target where the dominant therapeutic modality (small-molecule inhibitors, dozens of chemotypes) is **not antibodies**, and the reason is structural: the catalytic domain is membrane-anchored on the extracellular face but sits **behind the thick peptidoglycan cell wall**, which is porous to small molecules but restricts penetration of full-size IgG (~150 kDa). This is the central fact that organizes fields 4 (no anti-SrtA antibodies exist), 5 (no antibody epitopes mapped), 6 (antibody accessibility is the primary failure mode), 9 (epitope accessibility — buried vs exposed), 10 (zero antibody pipeline; rich small-molecule pipeline), and 11 (antibody approach is high-risk/low-probability). The field has correctly self-selected away from antibodies.

**Rule (generalizes to any cell-wall-anchored or membrane-proximal bacterial enzyme target):** when the target's catalytic/functional domain is shielded by the peptidoglycan layer (sortases, cell-wall synthesis enzymes, penicillin-binding proteins), document the accessibility barrier explicitly in fields 4, 6, 9, and 11. Do NOT list "no antibodies in development" as a mere gap — explain WHY (the structural barrier). For field 11, an antibody approach is high-risk unless a smaller format (VHH ~15 kDa, Fab ~50 kDa) can penetrate, and even that is speculative; the credible antibody-adjacent differentiation is a bispecific/ADC that targets an accessible sortase *substrate* surface protein (e.g., IsdB, ClfA) for bacterial binding while delivering a sortase-inhibiting or cell-wall-active payload. This is distinct from secreted-toxin targets (anthrax PA, PEA) where the antibody neutralizes a circulating factor and accessibility is not an issue, and from viral surface glycoproteins (EBOV GP, SUDV GP) where the target is solvent-exposed on the virion surface.

### 2. Anti-virulence (not bactericidal) target — the non-killing mechanism shapes field 6, field 8, and field 11

SrtA blockade strips S. aureus of its surface virulence repertoire but does not kill the bacterium. Small-molecule SrtA inhibitors protect mice from lethal bacteremia despite having no in vitro bactericidal activity (PMID 25197057). This is the defining mechanological distinction and it propagates: field 6 (failure mode — anti-virulence insufficiency in immunocompromised hosts or established infection), field 8 (no on-target host toxicity — no mammalian sortase ortholog), and field 11 (position as prophylaxis in high-risk patients, not treatment of established disease; combination with antibiotics is the credible deployment).

**Rule (extends the PcrV anti-virulence pattern):** for anti-virulence targets (sortase, T3SS structural proteins, quorum-sensing enzymes), field 2 must state "effect of blockade = loss of virulence function, not bacterial death." Field 6 must list "anti-virulence insufficiency in immunocompromised hosts" as a failure mode — clearance depends on host immunity or adjunct antibiotics. Field 8 (safety) is typically favorable (no host ortholog → no on-target toxicity) but the therapeutic index is limited by efficacy, not safety. Field 11 should position the asset as prophylaxis or adjunct, not monotherapy. This is a recurring pattern for the entire anti-virulence target class.

### 3. Enzyme target with rich structural coverage but zero antibody epitope data — the inverse of the secreted-toxin pattern

SrtA has extensive structural coverage (crystal structures of native enzyme, active-site mutant, and SrtA–LPETG substrate complex; NMR solution structure of the covalent thioacyl-intermediate analog; Ca²⁺-binding/β6/β7 loop dynamics) — enough to define the functionally critical inhibitory epitopes (active-site cleft, β6/β7 loop, lipid II binding site). But NO antibody has been raised against any of these epitopes, so field 5 (epitope landscape) is entirely inferred from structural biology, not from antibody mapping. This is the inverse of the secreted-toxin pattern (PEA, anthrax EF) where functional epitope mapping came from antibody characterization and no antibody–target complex structure existed in the PDB. For enzyme targets where the structural biology is mature but the antibody field is empty, field 5 should explicitly state which epitopes would be functionally inhibitory (based on the structural data) and flag that none has been validated by antibody binding.

**Rule:** when a target has rich structural data but no antibody epitope mapping, field 5 should: (a) list the functionally critical surfaces identified by structural biology (active site, allosteric sites, substrate-binding sites), (b) classify which would be "neutralizing" (inhibitory) if targeted, and (c) explicitly state "no antibody has been raised against these epitopes" rather than leaving the field empty. The structural data substitutes for epitope-mapping data — it tells you WHERE an antibody would need to bind, even though no antibody has been tested.

### 4. PubMed [tiab] exact-phrase queries return 0 for multi-word target names — broaden to boolean, and expect rate-limiting

The two narrowest queries (`"S. aureus sortase A antibody"[tiab]` and `"sortase A vaccine"[tiab]`) both returned 0 hits — the exact phrases never appear verbatim in any title/abstract. Broadening to boolean/multi-term variants (`"sortase A" "Staphylococcus aureus" virulence[tiab]` → 147 hits; `sortase A SrtA Staphylococcus aureus[tiab]` → 194 hits) recovered the literature. Two queries hit transient HTTP 429 rate limits; both succeeded on retry after a 4–8 s sleep. This confirms two established patterns simultaneously: (a) exact-phrase `[tiab]` queries fail for multi-word target names (the PEA, FGF19, FABP4 pattern) — always have boolean fallbacks ready; (b) NCBI rate-limits aggressive E-utilities use — implement retry-with-backoff (sleep 4–8 s between calls, retry on 429) as the default, not an afterthought.

**Rule (consolidates the rate-limit lesson across sessions):** when running ≥5 PubMed E-utilities queries in one session, (a) sleep 3–5 s between esearch calls (the task spec says 3–5; 4–5 is safer), (b) wrap every call in a retry loop (3–4 attempts, exponential backoff starting at 8 s), (c) batch esummary/efetch in groups of ≤25 PMIDs per call, and (d) keep boolean query variants pre-written so a zero-result narrow query can be replaced immediately without a second planning round. The combination of rate-limit handling + query-variant breadth is what made this session's 78-PMID retrieval robust despite two 429s.

### 5. UniProt REST JSON parsing — the nested-schema gotcha

UniProt REST JSON has deeply nested and sometimes optional fields. The initial parsing attempt crashed on `entry.get("organism", {}).get("names", [])` because `names` values were strings in some entries, not dicts. The fix: dump the raw JSON and inspect the actual structure before writing accessors, then use defensive `.get()` chains with type checks. The final working pattern: iterate `results`, extract `primaryAccession` directly, then walk `proteinDescription.recommendedName.names` (list of {type, value} dicts where type=="PRIMARY" gives the full name) and `organism.scientificName`. The EC number and supporting PubMed evidence codes are in `proteinDescription.recommendedName.ecNumbers`. UniProt confirmed Q2FV99 with EC 3.4.22.70 and 7 supporting PMIDs in a single call — grounding field 1 authoritatively.

**Rule (extends the UniProt-first pattern):** UniProt REST JSON field paths are not stable across entry types/versions — always `json.dumps` the first result and eyeball the structure before writing nested accessors. For target profiling, the high-value fields are: `primaryAccession`, `proteinDescription.recommendedName.fullName.value` / `.names[type=PRIMARY]`, `proteinDescription.recommendedName.ecNumbers[0].value`, `organism.scientificName`, `genes[0].geneName.value`, `sequence.length`, and `uniProtKBCrossReferences[database=PDB]` for the PDB inventory. The `ecNumbers.evidences` array even lists the supporting PMIDs — useful for cross-checking the literature search.
